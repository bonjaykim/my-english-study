Debating Vendor Modules and Upstream-First under the Generic Kernel Image

GKI 환경에서 벤더 모듈과 업스트림 우선 원칙 토론

금요 기술 공유 두 번째 세션으로, Atlas의 6.12 GKI 전환을 앞두고 Orion에서 가져온 약 300개의 아웃오브트리 커널 패치를 어떻게 처리할지 토론합니다. 커널·BSP, 플랫폼 프레임워크, SQE, 제품 보안 팀이 모듈화, 벤더 훅, 업스트림 기여의 장단점과 패치 분류 기준을 정합니다.

What GKI Changes

06 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Here's the short version. In the old model, every device maker took the kernel from the chipset vendor, added hundreds of their own changes, and built a kernel that was unique to each device. That gave us total freedom, but it meant that every security fix had to be merged and tested separately for every device. With GKI, there's one kernel binary, built by Google from a common source tree, and it's the same for every device on that kernel version.

07 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Everything that's specific to our hardware or our product has to live outside that core binary, in loadable vendor modules. And modules can only use a defined set of kernel functions and data structures, called the symbol list. If a module needs something that isn't on the list, we have to ask Google to add it, and they'll only agree if there's a good reason.

08 · Rachel KimPlatform Framework

So in practice, you lose the ability to change the core kernel directly. Any change you used to make inside the kernel now has to either become a module, go into the common tree, or disappear.

09 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Exactly. There's one more option, which is where most of the debate comes from: vendor hooks. Google has added hundreds of small hook points in the core kernel where a vendor module can attach its own code, for example to adjust a scheduling decision. They're a pragmatic escape hatch, but they're also controversial, because they let vendors keep doing things that arguably should be solved properly upstream.

10 · Jiho NamProduct Security

And from a security perspective, the big win is that Google can fix a vulnerability in the core kernel once, and every GKI device can pick it up quickly. In the old model, our median time to ship a kernel security fix on Orion was about forty days. Partners who are fully on GKI are doing it in under two weeks.

The Patch Inventory

11 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Sora, show them the numbers. That's where it gets real.

12 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

We're carrying three hundred and six out-of-tree patches from Orion. I went through every one of them over the last two weeks and sorted them into four buckets. First bucket: sixty-two patches are already covered by the 6.12 common tree, either because we upstreamed them or because upstream solved the same problem differently. Those we simply drop. That's the easy part.

13 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Second bucket: one hundred and forty-one patches are driver code that can move into vendor modules with modest effort. These are things like our display panel drivers, sensor drivers, and charging logic. Third bucket: fifty-four patches change core kernel behavior, mostly in the scheduler, memory management, and power, and the only way to keep them is through vendor hooks, or by getting the change accepted upstream. Fourth bucket: forty-nine patches that, honestly, I can't justify keeping. They're workarounds for old bugs, debugging aids, or features nobody uses anymore.

14 · Ethan YoonSQE

Forty-nine patches nobody needs? How did we end up carrying those for years?

15 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Because removing a patch feels risky, and keeping it feels free. Each time we moved to a new kernel, it was easier to rebase everything than to ask whether each patch still mattered. GKI is forcing us to ask the question we should have been asking all along.

16 · Rachel KimPlatform Framework

Before you drop them, though, I'd want to know if any of the framework relies on their behavior. We've been bitten before by a kernel 'cleanup' that removed a sysfs file one of our services was reading.

17 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

That's a very fair point, and it's exactly why I want a review from each team before we drop anything. I'll publish the list of forty-nine with a short description of each, and give everyone two weeks to object.

The Debate: Vendor Hooks or Upstream

18 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Now the interesting part: the fifty-four core patches. My position is pragmatic. For Atlas, we should move as many of them as possible onto vendor hooks. We have a launch date in March, and upstreaming a scheduler change can easily take six months or more, with no guarantee of success. Vendor hooks let us keep our tuning and ship on time.

19 · Jiho NamProduct Security

I'd push back on that. Every vendor hook is a place where our module code runs inside the most sensitive parts of the kernel. From a security standpoint, that's attack surface we own forever. And if Google changes or removes a hook in a future kernel, we're back to square one. Upstream-first may be slower, but it's the only approach that actually reduces our long-term burden.

20 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

In principle, I agree with you. In practice, 'upstream-first' sometimes means 'ship late'. I've seen teams submit a patch upstream, get into a six-month discussion about the right design, and end up with something that doesn't solve their original problem. I can't bet the Atlas launch on that.

21 · Rachel KimPlatform Framework

Can I offer a framework perspective? We had the same debate about platform changes years ago. What worked for us was a two-track approach: use the escape hatch to ship, but open the upstream discussion at the same time, with a deadline to retire the local change. If we never set a deadline, the temporary solution becomes permanent.

22 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

I like that. And honestly, not all fifty-four patches are equal. About twenty of them are small power or scheduling tweaks that upstream would probably accept quickly, maybe within a release or two. Those are worth upstreaming now. The other thirty-four are larger, more product-specific changes where vendor hooks are the realistic option.

23 · Jiho NamProduct Security

Then I'd propose a rule for the hooks: every vendor hook we use needs a named owner, a security review of the code that runs inside it, and an expiry date when we revisit whether it can go upstream. If nobody's willing to own it, it shouldn't exist.

24 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

I can live with that, as long as the security review doesn't become a bottleneck. If every hook waits three weeks for a review, we're back to shipping late.

25 · Jiho NamProduct Security

Fair. Most hooks are small. If your team sends them in batches with a short design note, I can commit to a one-week turnaround.

Testing Implications

26 · Ethan YoonSQE

Let me bring this back to testing. If the core kernel comes from Google and gets updated more frequently, does SQE need to retest everything every time the core kernel changes? Because that would be a lot of testing.

27 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

That's one of the trade-offs. The core kernel changes are tested heavily by Google and by other partners using the same binary, so they're less risky than our own changes used to be. But our modules interact with the core kernel, so we still need a solid regression suite. My suggestion is a smaller kernel-focused suite that runs on every core kernel update, and the full suite only for our own module changes and monthly releases.

28 · Ethan YoonSQE

That's reasonable, but I'd want the kernel-focused suite to include our stress profiles, not just functional tests. Most of the kernel bugs we find, like the storage panic this month, only show up under sustained load.

29 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Agreed. A twelve-hour stress run on fifty devices would be a good minimum. Tom already proposed something similar for storage changes after the Orion incident, so we can reuse that setup.

30 · Rachel KimPlatform Framework

One question from me: will framework engineers still be able to debug kernel issues the same way? If the core kernel is a Google binary, do we still get symbols and source for crash analysis?

31 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

Yes. Google publishes the source and the debug symbols for every GKI build, so ramdump analysis works exactly the same way. The only difference is that if the bug is in the core kernel, the fix goes to Google rather than into our tree.

Managing the Symbol List

32 · Ethan YoonSQE

Sora, you mentioned that modules can only use functions on the symbol list. What happens when one of our modules needs something that isn't on the list, and we're two weeks from a milestone?

33 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

That's the scenario that worries me most. Google updates the symbol list on a regular schedule, and requests are reviewed in batches. If we miss a window, we may have to wait several weeks. The way to avoid that is to identify every symbol our modules need early, ideally right after we convert the driver patches, and submit them all at once.

34 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Kevin from Google also warned us about this on the Arvenna call. Partners who accumulate private symbol exports during bring-up end up spending weeks cleaning them up before certification. So we should build a check into our CI that fails any module using a symbol outside the official list, from day one.

35 · Jiho NamProduct Security

From a security angle, I'd add one more thing. Some symbols give modules very powerful access, for example to modify page tables or credentials. When our modules request those, I'd like to know, because a vulnerability in a module with that kind of access is much more dangerous than one in a simple sensor driver.

36 · Sora HanKernel & BSP

That's easy to add. I can flag sensitive symbols in the CI report, and any new request for one of them would need Jiho's approval before we submit it to Google.

37 · Rachel KimPlatform Framework

This sounds a lot like how we manage hidden platform APIs on the framework side. We keep an allow list, every exception needs a justification, and the list gets reviewed every release. It's tedious, but it's saved us from some very painful upgrades.

38 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

That's a good model to borrow. Let's treat the symbol list the same way, with a review at every kernel update.

Agreed Principles

44 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Let me try to capture what we agreed. One: the sixty-two patches already covered upstream are dropped. Two: the hundred and forty-one driver patches become vendor modules. Three: the forty-nine unjustified patches are published for a two-week objection period, then dropped. Four: of the fifty-four core patches, about twenty go upstream, and the rest use vendor hooks, each with an owner, a security review, and an expiry date. Five: a kernel-focused regression suite with stress runs for every core kernel update.

45 · Jiho NamProduct Security

GKI가 바꾸는 것

정확합니다. 선택지가 하나 더 있는데, 토론의 대부분이 거기서 나옵니다. 벤더 훅이요. Google은 핵심 커널에 벤더 모듈이 자체 코드를 붙일 수 있는 작은 훅 지점을 수백 개 추가했습니다. 예를 들어 스케줄링 결정을 조정하는 데 쓰입니다. 실용적인 탈출구이긴 하지만, 업스트림에서 제대로 해결해야 할 일을 벤더들이 계속하게 해 준다는 점에서 논란이 있습니다.

패치 현황

11 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

토론: 벤더 훅이냐 업스트림이냐

그 부분엔 이의를 제기하고 싶습니다. 벤더 훅 하나하나가 저희 모듈 코드가 커널의 가장 민감한 부분 안에서 실행되는 지점입니다. 보안 관점에서 그건 저희가 영원히 떠안는 공격 표면이에요. 그리고 Google이 향후 커널에서 훅을 바꾸거나 없애면 원점으로 돌아갑니다. 업스트림 우선은 느릴 수 있지만, 장기적인 부담을 실제로 줄이는 유일한 방법입니다.
