Tracking Down a Hard Lockup during Fold and Unfold Transitions

폴딩·언폴딩 전환 중 발생하는 hard lockup 추적

Symptom and Test Setup

06 · Hyemi SongSQE

Here's what we know. We have six folding robots that open and close devices continuously, about four times a minute, while running a mix of apps. Over the last week, we ran just over forty thousand fold cycles. We had a hundred and twenty-two freezes, so about 0.3 percent per fold. The screen freezes in the middle of the transition, and about ten seconds later the device reboots.

07 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

Point three percent per fold sounds small, but a typical user folds the device around eighty times a day. That would mean a freeze roughly every four days for every user. That's clearly a launch blocker.

08 · Hyemi SongSQE

Exactly. And the hardest part is the reset reason. In every single case, it's a watchdog bite, not a bark. So we have no ramdump. All we have is the last kernel log that survives in the preserved memory region.

09 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

As Yuna explained at the tech share last week, a bite usually means the CPU couldn't even handle the bark interrupt. In display code, the most common cause I know of is a CPU getting stuck on a register access, not in a software loop.

10 · Hyemi SongSQE

Can you explain that for those of us who aren't hardware people? How can reading a register make a CPU hang?

11 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

I can take that one. When the CPU reads a register in the display controller, the request travels across the chip's interconnect to that block. If the block's clock is turned off or its power domain is collapsed, nobody answers the request. On some chips, the CPU then waits forever for a response that never comes. It's stuck at the hardware level, so it can't take interrupts, and the watchdog eventually bites.

Reading the Last Kernel Log

12 · Hyemi SongSQE

Woojin, what do the last kernel logs show?

13 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

We have usable logs from ninety-eight of the hundred and twenty-two freezes. In every one, the last messages are from the display switching sequence. The pattern is consistent: the main display pipeline starts powering up, the cover display pipeline starts powering down, and then the log simply stops. The last line is usually our debug message saying the cover pipeline's power domain is being turned off.

14 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

That fits the theory perfectly. If something tries to read a cover pipeline register right after its power domain goes off, the CPU would hang exactly at that point. The question is what would touch the cover pipeline after we've decided to turn it off.

15 · Takeshi MoriLumina Panel Engineering

Could it be related to the panel's tearing effect signal? Our panels send a signal at the start of each frame, and your driver uses it to time the next update. If the cover panel sends one last signal while it's shutting down, the interrupt handler might try to read the controller's status.

16 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

That's a very good thought. The interrupt handler does read a status register to check which frame completed. And it runs on whichever CPU receives the interrupt, independently of the switching sequence. So there's a window where the handler could fire after the power-off has started.

17 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

But we disable that interrupt before powering down. That's the first thing the power-off sequence does. I checked that code carefully when I wrote it.

18 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

Disabling the interrupt stops new interrupts from being delivered, but it doesn't wait for a handler that's already running on another CPU. If the tearing signal arrived a few microseconds before you disabled it, the handler could still be in progress on CPU two while CPU zero turns off the power. You'd need to synchronize, to wait for any in-flight handler to finish, before collapsing the domain.

19 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

That's a classic mistake, and I think you're right. Let me check whether we call the synchronization function after disabling the interrupt. If we don't, that's almost certainly our bug.

Getting Better Evidence

20 · Hyemi SongSQE

This is a promising theory, but we're still guessing from the last line of a log. Is there any way to turn this kind of hang into something we can actually see in a dump?

21 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

Yes. Our interconnect has a debug feature called access timeout detection. When it's enabled, if a request to a powered-off block doesn't get a response within a set time, the interconnect returns an error to the CPU instead of letting it wait forever. The kernel then gets a bus error exception, which normally leads to a panic with a full register dump, including the exact address that was being accessed.

22 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

That would be perfect. We'd go from guessing to knowing exactly which register and which code path caused it. Why isn't it enabled by default?

23 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

It adds a small amount of latency to every access through the interconnect, and in production, some customers prefer a silent hang and reboot over a panic with a dump. But for debugging, it's invaluable. I'd recommend enabling it on the robot devices right away. I'll send the configuration this afternoon.

24 · Hyemi SongSQE

Great. With six robots and a freeze every three hundred folds or so, we should see several dumps within a day. I'll also keep two robots on the original configuration as a control, so we can confirm the timeout detection doesn't change the failure rate in some unexpected way.

25 · Takeshi MoriLumina Panel Engineering

On our side, I'd like to measure the timing of the tearing signal during shutdown. I suspect our panel may send one more signal than your driver expects, about sixteen milliseconds after the display-off command. If that's true, it widens the window for the race.

26 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

That's useful, but I'd be careful not to treat that as the root cause. Even if the panel sends an extra signal, our driver should handle it safely. The fix has to be on our side regardless.

27 · Takeshi MoriLumina Panel Engineering

I agree completely. I'm just trying to explain why it happens so often, so you can test the fix under realistic timing.

Fix Options

28 · Hyemi SongSQE

Let's assume for a moment that the theory is confirmed by tomorrow. What does the fix look like, and how risky is it?

29 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

There are two layers again. First, after disabling the interrupt, we call the synchronization function so any in-flight handler finishes before we power down. That closes the race. Second, as defense in depth, the interrupt handler should check a flag that says the pipeline is powering down, and return immediately without touching any registers.

30 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

Both changes are small. Maybe thirty lines total. The risk is in timing: the synchronization call can wait a few hundred microseconds if a handler is running, which slightly extends the fold transition. I don't expect anyone to notice, but we should measure it.

31 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

I'd add a third check. Before any register access in the display driver, verify that the power domain is actually on, at least in debug builds. Our reference driver has a helper for this. It won't run in production, but it will catch similar mistakes in other code paths during development.

32 · Hyemi SongSQE

I like that a lot. It turns a silent hardware hang into a loud software warning, which is exactly what we want during development.

Fallback Options and Launch Communication

38 · Hyemi SongSQE

I also need a fallback before I talk to the launch team on Monday. If the theory turns out to be wrong, or the fix doesn't hold up on the robots, what's our plan B? I don't want to walk into that meeting with only one option.

39 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

The bluntest option would be to delay powering down the cover pipeline. Instead of turning it off immediately when the main screen comes on, we keep it powered for an extra hundred milliseconds. Any late tearing signal or in-flight handler would finish while the block is still alive. It costs a little power on each fold, but it would almost certainly hide the problem.

40 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

'Hide' is the right word, though. A delay narrows the window, but it doesn't close it. Under heavy load, a handler could still be delayed by more than a hundred milliseconds, and you'd be back to a hang, just much more rarely, which would make it even harder to debug in the field.

41 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

Agreed. I'd only use the delay together with the real fix, never instead of it. But as a fallback for the factory build, if the synchronization fix shows any side effects, a delay is a much better outcome than a freeze every four days.

42 · Hyemi SongSQE

So for the launch team, I'll present it like this: plan A is the synchronization fix plus the handler check, validated on the robots by the twenty-fifth. Plan B adds the power-off delay as an extra margin if plan A shows anything unexpected. In both cases, we meet the August 28th cutoff.

43 · Takeshi MoriLumina Panel Engineering

If it helps, we can also adjust the panel's shutdown sequence in a future panel firmware update, so it stops sending tearing signals immediately after the display-off command. It won't make the factory build, but it would remove one of the triggers for later production batches.

44 · Hyemi SongSQE

That's a good long-term improvement. Let's track it separately so it doesn't distract from the factory build. Takeshi, could you open a ticket on your side and share the number with Woojin, so both teams are following the same item?

Other Possible Instances

45 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

One more suggestion. This pattern, disabling an interrupt without synchronizing before powering down a block, is easy to repeat in other drivers. It might be worth a quick audit of the camera, video, and touch drivers on Orion Fold, since they also power blocks up and down frequently.

Good point. A simple search for places where we disable an interrupt and then turn off a power domain or clock in the same function would find most candidates. I'll ask Junho's camera team and the touch team to look at their drivers this week.

증상과 테스트 환경

06 · Hyemi SongSQE

정확합니다. 그리고 가장 어려운 부분은 리셋 원인입니다. 모든 경우에 bark가 아니라 워치독 bite입니다. 그래서 램덤프가 없습니다. 보존된 메모리 영역에 남은 마지막 커널 로그가 가진 전부입니다.

지난주 기술 공유에서 유나 님이 설명했듯이, bite는 보통 CPU가 bark 인터럽트조차 처리하지 못했다는 뜻입니다. 디스플레이 코드에서 제가 아는 가장 흔한 원인은 소프트웨어 루프가 아니라 레지스터 접근에서 CPU가 멈추는 경우입니다.

10 · Hyemi SongSQE

하드웨어 쪽이 아닌 사람들을 위해 설명해 주실 수 있나요? 레지스터를 읽는 게 어떻게 CPU를 멈추게 할 수 있죠?

11 · Lena HoffmannQorvix Display Engineering

그건 제가 설명할게요. CPU가 디스플레이 컨트롤러의 레지스터를 읽으면, 그 요청은 칩의 인터커넥트를 거쳐 해당 블록으로 갑니다. 블록의 클럭이 꺼져 있거나 전원 도메인이 내려가 있으면 아무도 요청에 응답하지 않습니다. 일부 칩에서는 CPU가 끝내 오지 않는 응답을 영원히 기다립니다. 하드웨어 수준에서 멈춘 거라 인터럽트를 받을 수 없고, 결국 워치독이 무는 거죠.

마지막 커널 로그 읽기

12 · Hyemi SongSQE

우진 님, 마지막 커널 로그에는 뭐가 나오나요?

13 · Woojin ChoiDisplay Software

멈춤 122건 중 98건에서 쓸 만한 로그를 얻었습니다. 모든 경우에 마지막 메시지가 디스플레이 전환 시퀀스에서 나옵니다. 패턴은 일관됩니다. 메인 디스플레이 파이프라인이 전원을 켜기 시작하고, 커버 디스플레이 파이프라인이 전원을 끄기 시작하고, 그다음 로그가 그냥 멈춥니다. 마지막 줄은 보통 커버 파이프라인의 전원 도메인을 끄고 있다는 저희 디버그 메시지입니다.

14 · Jisu HanDisplay Software

그게 가설과 딱 맞아떨어집니다. 커버 파이프라인의 전원 도메인이 꺼진 직후 누군가 그 레지스터를 읽으려고 하면, 정확히 그 지점에서 CPU가 멈출 겁니다. 문제는 저희가 끄기로 결정한 후에 무엇이 커버 파이프라인을 건드리느냐입니다.

패널의 티어링 이펙트 신호와 관련이 있을 수 있을까요? 저희 패널은 각 프레임 시작 때 신호를 보내고, 귀사 드라이버는 그걸로 다음 업데이트 타이밍을 맞춥니다. 커버 패널이 꺼지는 중에 마지막 신호를 하나 보내면, 인터럽트 핸들러가 컨트롤러 상태를 읽으려고 할 수 있습니다.

그런데 저희는 전원을 끄기 전에 그 인터럽트를 비활성화합니다. 전원 끄기 시퀀스가 제일 먼저 하는 일이에요. 작성할 때 그 코드를 꼼꼼히 확인했습니다.

인터럽트를 비활성화하면 새 인터럽트 전달은 막지만, 이미 다른 CPU에서 실행 중인 핸들러를 기다려 주지는 않습니다. 비활성화하기 몇 마이크로초 전에 티어링 신호가 도착했다면, 0번 CPU가 전원을 끄는 동안 2번 CPU에서 핸들러가 여전히 진행 중일 수 있습니다. 도메인을 내리기 전에 동기화해서, 진행 중인 핸들러가 끝날 때까지 기다려야 합니다.

전형적인 실수네요. 말씀이 맞는 것 같습니다. 인터럽트를 비활성화한 뒤에 동기화 함수를 호출하는지 확인해 볼게요. 호출하지 않는다면 거의 확실히 저희 버그입니다.
