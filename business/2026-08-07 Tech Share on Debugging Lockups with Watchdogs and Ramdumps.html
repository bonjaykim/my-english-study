Types of Lockups

07 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Great, Elena's question is a perfect starting point. Let's define our terms, because people use 'hang' to mean five different things. At the bottom, we have a hard lockup: a CPU is stuck with interrupts disabled, so it can't even respond to the timer. Above that, a soft lockup: a CPU is stuck in kernel code with interrupts enabled, but it hasn't let the scheduler run anything else for more than twenty seconds, by default.

08 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Then there's a hung task, which is different. The CPU is fine, but a specific task has been sleeping in an uninterruptible state for too long, usually waiting for a lock or for I/O that never completes. The CPU is happily running other things. It's just that one task, and everything waiting for it, is stuck.

09 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

And the system server watchdog sits on top of all that, right? When our watchdog fires, it's because a thread in the system server didn't check in for sixty seconds.

10 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Exactly. And here's the key insight: a framework watchdog can be caused by any of the layers below it. If a kernel driver holds a mutex forever, the system server thread that needs it shows up as a hung task, and sixty seconds later, the framework watchdog fires. The framework is the messenger, not necessarily the culprit.

11 · Tom AhnSQE

Can I ask about the hardware watchdog? In our reset reasons, we see 'watchdog bark' and 'watchdog bite'. I've always assumed they're the same thing.

12 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Good question, and it's a common confusion. The hardware watchdog is a timer that the kernel has to pet regularly. If it isn't petted in time, first it barks: it raises an interrupt, and the kernel gets one last chance to save its state, usually by triggering a panic and collecting a ramdump. If the kernel can't even handle that interrupt, because the CPU is completely locked, the watchdog bites: it resets the chip immediately, and you often get very little information.

13 · Tom AhnSQE

So a bite is a much worse sign than a bark, from a debugging point of view.

14 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

In terms of how much data you get, yes. A bite usually means interrupts were disabled on the CPU that should have handled the bark. So the reset reason alone already tells you to look for code that disables interrupts for too long, which narrows things down a lot.

15 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

Is there any way to get more information out of a bite? It feels like those are the cases where we need it most.

16 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

There are a couple of tricks. On most chipsets, the bootloader can preserve a small region of memory across the reset, and the kernel writes its log there continuously. So after a bite, the next boot can still recover the last few seconds of kernel messages. It's not a full dump, but it often shows which driver was active right before the reset. We call it the last kernel log, and SQE should always attach it.

17 · Tom AhnSQE

Noted. I'll make that mandatory in our filing template.

Walking Through a Real Ramdump

18 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Let me make this concrete with a real case from Orion last spring. The symptom was that the screen froze during a video call, and after about sixty seconds, the device rebooted. SQE filed it as a display issue, and the display team spent a week on it. When I finally looked at the dump, here's the order I followed, and I'd recommend everyone use the same order.

19 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Step one: read the panic reason and the last kernel log lines. In this case, the panic was triggered by the framework watchdog, which killed the system server, and the log said the main thread was blocked. That tells us the framework detected the problem, but not who caused it. Step two: look at what the blocked thread is waiting on. Here, it was waiting in the kernel, inside the binder driver, for a reply from the camera hardware service.

20 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

So you follow the chain of who is waiting for whom, until you find the thread that isn't waiting for anything but is still stuck.

21 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Exactly, that's step three. The camera service thread was waiting on a mutex in the camera driver. The crash utility can tell you the owner of a mutex, so I looked up the owner, and it was a kernel worker thread that was running, not sleeping, on CPU five. When I looked at that CPU's stack, it was spinning in a loop inside the image processor driver, waiting for a hardware status bit that never got set.

22 · Tom AhnSQE

So in the end, it was the camera driver, not the display. The display just stopped updating because the whole framework was blocked behind it.

23 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

Right. And the fix was a timeout on that polling loop plus a hardware reset path for the image processor. The display team had done nothing wrong. With the right order, that triage takes about fifteen minutes. Without it, it took a week and three teams.

24 · Seojin LimModem Software

A quick question about step two. Binder transactions cross process boundaries. How do you find which thread in the other process is handling the call? In the modem service, we've had cases where we couldn't figure that out.

25 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

The binder driver keeps a record of every outstanding transaction, including the source and target threads. In the dump, you can print the binder state, and it shows something like: thread A in process X is waiting for transaction number such-and-such, which is being handled by thread B in process Y. It's one of the most useful things in the whole dump, and very few people know it's there.

26 · Seojin LimModem Software

That would have saved us days last month. Could you add the exact command to the wiki page?

27 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Absolutely. That's actually one of today's action items.

Debate: Panic on Soft Lockup in User Builds

28 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Now for the discussion part. There's a configuration option that makes the kernel panic, and therefore reboot, as soon as a soft lockup is detected. In our engineering builds, it's on. In user builds, the ones customers get, it's off. Yuna and I disagree on whether it should be on for user builds, and we thought it would be fun to argue about it in public.

29 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

My position is that it should be on. Today, when a soft lockup happens on a customer device, the kernel prints a warning and keeps going. Sometimes the device recovers, but often it ends up half-dead: the screen is frozen, the user has to hold the power button, and we get no dump at all. If we panicked immediately, we'd collect a ramdump, the device would reboot in about a minute, and we'd actually be able to fix the root cause.

30 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

And my position is that it's too aggressive for customers. Not every soft lockup is fatal. Some are caused by very long but legitimate operations, like a huge memory compaction on a device under pressure. In those cases, the system recovers on its own after a few seconds. If we panic, we turn a short stutter into a full reboot, which is far worse for the user. A reboot also shows up in our quality metrics, while a stutter doesn't.

31 · Tom AhnSQE

Isn't that last point a bit of a red flag, though? If we're choosing a policy partly because it keeps a metric looking good, we might be hiding real problems from ourselves.

32 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Fair challenge, and I'll take that back. The metric shouldn't drive the decision. The user experience should. And for the user, a two-second stutter is better than a sixty-second reboot.

33 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

From the framework side, I'd add that the half-dead state Yuna described is the worst outcome of all. When the kernel recovers partially, the system server often ends up in a weird state, and we see strange bugs for hours afterward. A clean reboot is sometimes kinder than limping along.

34 · Seojin LimModem Software

Is there a middle ground? For example, don't panic on the first soft lockup, but if the same CPU is still stuck after, say, sixty seconds, then panic. Short stalls recover on their own, and real lockups get a dump.

35 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

I actually like that a lot. It addresses Daniel's concern about legitimate long operations, and it addresses my concern about half-dead devices. We'd need to check whether the kernel supports that kind of escalation out of the box, or whether we'd have to carry a patch.

36 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

I can live with that, as long as we roll it out carefully. Let's not change the policy on all devices at once. We could enable it on the internal dogfood population first, compare the reboot rate and the number of useful dumps for a month, and then decide based on data rather than on who argued more convincingly today.

37 · Tom AhnSQE

SQE would be happy to help with the comparison. We can mirror the setting in the stress lab too, which would give us a much faster signal than dogfood alone.

Tooling and Data Collection

38 · Seojin LimModem Software

Before we close, I want to go back to my original reason for being here. After our incident, I'm nervous about any debug-related setting reaching a production build. How do we make sure the ramdump collection policy itself is correct for each build type?

39 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Good question. In user builds, a full ramdump is disabled, because it can contain personal data, and it's too big to upload anyway. What we collect instead is a minidump: only the kernel log, the registers and stacks of each CPU, and a few key data structures, with all user memory excluded. It's usually a few megabytes, and in my experience, it's enough to triage about seventy percent of lockups.

40 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

And for the remaining thirty percent, we usually need the lock owner information, which isn't in the minidump today. That's something I'd like to add: the list of tasks in uninterruptible sleep and what each one is waiting on. It's small, it doesn't contain user data, and it would have made the camera case I described solvable from a minidump alone.

41 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

If you add that, please include the binder transaction state as well. Half of the framework hangs I see are blocked binder calls, and that's exactly the information that would point me to the right process.

42 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Noted. We'll need a privacy review for any change to what's collected, even if it's just kernel metadata, so let's propose both additions together in one review.

락업의 종류

07 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

좋습니다. Elena의 질문이 시작점으로 딱 좋네요. 용어부터 정의합시다. 사람들이 'hang'을 다섯 가지 다른 의미로 쓰거든요. 가장 아래에는 hard lockup이 있습니다. CPU가 인터럽트가 비활성화된 상태로 멈춰서 타이머에도 응답할 수 없는 상태입니다. 그 위에 soft lockup이 있는데, CPU가 인터럽트는 활성화된 상태로 커널 코드에 갇혀서, 기본값 기준으로 20초 넘게 스케줄러가 다른 걸 실행하지 못하게 하는 상태입니다.

08 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

그리고 hung task가 있는데, 이건 다릅니다. CPU는 정상인데, 특정 태스크가 너무 오랫동안 인터럽트 불가능한 상태로 잠들어 있는 겁니다. 보통 락이나 끝나지 않는 I/O를 기다리고 있죠. CPU는 다른 일을 잘 실행하고 있습니다. 그 태스크 하나와 그걸 기다리는 모든 것만 멈춰 있는 거죠.

09 · Elena ParkPlatform Framework

그리고 시스템 서버 워치독은 그 모든 것 위에 있는 거죠? 저희 워치독이 발동하는 건 시스템 서버 안의 스레드가 60초 동안 응답을 보고하지 않았기 때문이고요.

10 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

정확합니다. 그리고 핵심 통찰은 이겁니다. 프레임워크 워치독은 그 아래 어느 계층에 의해서도 발생할 수 있습니다. 커널 드라이버가 뮤텍스를 영원히 잡고 있으면, 그게 필요한 시스템 서버 스레드는 hung task로 나타나고, 60초 뒤 프레임워크 워치독이 발동합니다. 프레임워크는 소식을 전하는 쪽이지, 반드시 범인은 아닙니다.

11 · Tom AhnSQE

하드웨어 워치독에 대해 물어봐도 될까요? 리셋 원인에 'watchdog bark'와 'watchdog bite'가 보이는데, 저는 항상 같은 거라고 생각했거든요.

12 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

좋은 질문이고, 흔히 헷갈리는 부분입니다. 하드웨어 워치독은 커널이 주기적으로 쓰다듬어 줘야 하는 타이머입니다. 제때 쓰다듬지 않으면 먼저 짖습니다. 즉 bark는 인터럽트를 발생시키고, 커널은 상태를 저장할 마지막 기회를 얻는데, 보통 패닉을 일으키고 램덤프를 수집합니다. CPU가 완전히 잠겨서 그 인터럽트조차 처리하지 못하면 워치독이 뭅니다. bite는 칩을 즉시 리셋하고, 정보가 거의 남지 않는 경우가 많습니다.

13 · Tom AhnSQE

실제 램덤프 분석 따라가기

18 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

19 · Yuna KwakKernel & BSP

1단계는 패닉 원인과 마지막 커널 로그 줄을 읽는 겁니다. 이 경우 패닉은 프레임워크 워치독이 시스템 서버를 종료하면서 발생했고, 로그에는 메인 스레드가 블록됐다고 나와 있었습니다. 프레임워크가 문제를 감지했다는 건 알 수 있지만, 누가 원인인지는 알 수 없습니다. 2단계는 블록된 스레드가 무엇을 기다리는지 보는 겁니다. 여기서는 커널 안 binder 드라이버에서 카메라 하드웨어 서비스의 응답을 기다리고 있었습니다.
