Code Review Standards and the Upstream-First Debate

코드 리뷰 기준과 업스트림 우선 정책 토론

월간 기술 공유 세션에서 커널·BSP, 프레임워크, 제품 보안, 릴리스 관리팀이 코드 리뷰 품질과 커널 변경의 업스트림 우선 정책을 두고 토론합니다. 리베이스 비용, 출시 속도, 제품 정보 노출, 보안 수정 예외, 커밋 메시지 품질을 논의하고 Atlas 커널에서 3개월 파일럿을 진행하기로 합니다.

01 / English script

Opening

01 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Good afternoon, everyone. This is our monthly tech-sharing session, and today's topic is one that people have strong feelings about: how we review code, and whether we should adopt an upstream-first policy for kernel changes. I'll share some data first, and then I'd like a real debate. Nobody needs to agree with me.

02 · Grace JungRelease Management

I'm here mostly because every out-of-tree patch becomes my problem at rebase time. So I'm biased, but I'll try to listen.

03 · Jiho NamProduct Security

And I'm here because review is the last line of defense before a vulnerability ships. I want to make sure security doesn't get lost in a conversation about process.

04 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Perfect. Those are exactly the perspectives I was hoping for.

Where We Are Today

05 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Let's start with the numbers. Our Orion kernel carries about eleven hundred patches on top of the Android common kernel. Of those, roughly two hundred are vendor drivers that will never go upstream. The rest are ours: fixes, tuning, and features. Only about forty of them have ever been sent upstream.

06 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

And each rebase costs us. When we moved from six-one to six-six for the internal test branch, three engineers spent five weeks resolving conflicts. About a third of those conflicts were in patches that fixed bugs upstream had already fixed in a different way.

07 · Brian ShinFramework

So we were fixing the same bug twice, and then paying again to throw our version away.

08 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Exactly. And nobody noticed, because our patch never went outside the building.

09 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

On the review side, our median kernel review takes about six hours from upload to approval. That sounds fast, but in a sample of fifty reviews, thirty-one had only one comment or none. Upstream, the same kind of patch typically gets several rounds of feedback.

10 · Elena ParkFramework

To be fair, a review with no comments isn't automatically a bad review. Sometimes the patch is just good.

11 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

That's fair. But when I traced the eight regressions from the last quarter back to their commits, six of them had been approved with no comments at all.

The Case for Upstream First

12 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Let me make the case. Upstream first means that before a generic kernel fix lands in our tree, it's posted to the upstream mailing list. We don't have to wait for it to be merged, but it has to be posted, and the link goes into our commit message.

13 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Three things happen when you do that. First, you get reviewed by the people who wrote the code you're changing. Second, if upstream already has a better fix, someone tells you within days instead of us discovering it during a rebase two years later. And third, once it's merged, the patch disappears from our pile forever.

14 · Grace JungRelease Management

That third point is the one I care about. Every patch we carry is a patch somebody has to carry again on the next kernel.

15 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Google is also moving in this direction. For GKI, they already reject vendor hooks that don't have a clear upstream story. So in some sense the decision is being made for us anyway.

The Case Against

16 · Brian ShinFramework

I'll take the other side, because someone should. My worry is speed. When we have a launch-blocking bug, I need the fix in the build tonight, not after a public discussion that can take weeks. Upstream reviewers don't care about our launch date.

17 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

But that's not what the policy says. You post it and land it the same day. The upstream review runs in parallel. If they ask for changes, we pick up the improved version later.

18 · Brian ShinFramework

Then what do we gain if we land it anyway? We still carry the patch until upstream accepts it.

19 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

We gain the feedback, and we gain a deadline for the patch to stop being ours. Right now there's no such deadline, so patches live forever.

20 · Elena ParkFramework

My concern is different. Some of our changes reveal product plans. If we post a patch that adds support for a new display mode three months before launch, people can read it and guess what we're building.

21 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

That's a legitimate concern, and it's why I'd scope the policy to generic fixes, not new hardware features. A locking bug in the scheduler doesn't reveal anything about our roadmap.

The Security Angle

22 · Jiho NamProduct Security

Here's where I need to push back on everyone. Security fixes are a special case. If we find a vulnerability in the kernel, posting the fix publicly before devices are patched tells attackers exactly where to look. That has to go through the embargoed security process, not the public list.

23 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Completely agree. The policy would explicitly exclude anything that goes through the security team. Those follow the coordinated disclosure timeline.

24 · Jiho NamProduct Security

Good. My second point is about our internal reviews. I'd like any change that touches parsing of untrusted input, memory permissions, or the boundary between user space and the kernel to require a second reviewer from a different team. One comment-free approval isn't enough for those areas.

25 · Brian ShinFramework

How would people know when a change falls into that category? Engineers don't always realize their patch touches a security boundary.

26 · Jiho NamProduct Security

We can do it by path. We already have a list of sensitive directories and files. The review tool can add a security reviewer automatically when a patch touches one of them.

What a Good Review Looks Like

27 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Let's talk about review quality itself, because upstream first only covers part of the tree. What do we actually expect from a reviewer?

28 · Elena ParkFramework

For me, the reviewer should be able to explain the change to someone else. If you can't explain why the patch is correct, you shouldn't approve it. And the commit message should make that easy: what was broken, why, and how this fixes it.

29 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Upstream maintainers are brutal about commit messages, and honestly, they're right. Half of our internal commit messages just say 'fix crash' with a ticket number. Two years later, the ticket system has been migrated and nobody knows what the crash was.

30 · Grace JungRelease Management

I've lived that. During the last rebase, I spent a whole day trying to figure out whether a patch was still needed, and the only clue was a ticket link that returned an error.

31 · Brian ShinFramework

Fine, I can get behind better commit messages. That's cheap and it helps everyone. But I don't want reviews to become a ritual where people leave comments just to prove they read the code.

32 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Agreed. The goal isn't more comments. The goal is that the reviewer actually understood the change. A short note saying what you checked is often enough, for example, 'verified the lock is held on all callers.'

Cost and Capacity

33 · Grace JungRelease Management

Let me ask the practical question. Who's going to do this? Posting upstream means responding to feedback, sending new versions, sometimes arguing for weeks. Our engineers are already fully loaded.

34 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

I won't pretend it's free. My estimate is about ten percent of a kernel engineer's time. But compare that with five weeks for three people at every rebase. Over two years, it's cheaper to do it upstream.

35 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

There's also a hiring and growth benefit. Engineers who have patches in the mainline kernel get better at writing code that others can review. That skill comes back into our internal reviews.

36 · Brian ShinFramework

I'll concede that point. Some of the best reviewers on my team learned by getting torn apart on a public list.

37 · Elena ParkFramework

Can I add one practical suggestion? If we make upstream work visible, people will treat it as real work. Right now, nobody gets credit for a patch that's merged upstream, because it doesn't show up in our project tracking at all. If it appeared in performance reviews the same way a feature does, I think engineers would volunteer for it.

38 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

That's a great point, and it costs almost nothing. I'll talk to the engineering managers about adding upstream contributions to the quarterly goals, starting with the people in the pilot.

A Recent Example

39 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Let me make this concrete with a case from July. Some of you will remember the Orion Lite camera hang. The root cause was a patch in the workqueue code that changed how a flush was handled. It was approved in under two hours with no comments.

40 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

And here's the painful part. The same race had been discussed on the upstream list eight months earlier. A maintainer explained exactly why the obvious fix was wrong, and the correct fix was already in a newer kernel. If our engineer had posted the patch, someone would have pointed to that thread within a day.

41 · Brian ShinFramework

How long did the hang take to fix in the end?

42 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Three weeks from the first field report to the fix, plus a maintenance release, plus the SQE time to reproduce it. Compare that with the five minutes it would have taken to send an email to the mailing list. I'm not saying every case is like this, but this one cost us a lot.

43 · Elena ParkFramework

That's a strong example, but I want to be careful about one thing. The engineer who wrote that patch was new, and the reviewer was overloaded that week. I don't want this discussion to turn into blaming individuals. The process let them down.

44 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Completely agree, and that's why I didn't put any names on the slide. The question is what process would have caught it, not who should have caught it.

Tooling That Helps

45 · Jiho NamProduct Security

Before we talk about policy, I think tools can take a lot of the load off reviewers. Right now, our review system runs the build and a basic boot test, and that's it. Upstream, every patch is checked for style, and many subsystems run static analysis automatically.

46 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

We could add the upstream style checker and a sparse run to every kernel review in about a week. Neither of them finds deep bugs, but they remove the boring comments, so the human reviewer can focus on the logic.

47 · Brian ShinFramework

On the framework side, we already have lint checks, and honestly they changed the culture. People stopped arguing about formatting and started arguing about design, which is what we actually want.

48 · Jiho NamProduct Security

I'd also like lockdep and the kernel address sanitizer enabled on the nightly debug builds for Atlas. They catch exactly the kind of locking and memory bugs that slip through a quick review.

49 · Grace JungRelease Management

As long as they stay on debug builds, I have no objection. The performance cost would be unacceptable on user builds, but nightly builds are exactly the right place.

Working with Vendors

50 · Grace JungRelease Management

One more angle we haven't covered is vendor code. Qorvix and Arvenna send us large drops of kernel patches. Do we hold them to the same standard?

51 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

We should, at least for generic fixes. When I looked at the last Qorvix drop, about sixty patches had nothing to do with their hardware. They were fixes to the memory manager and the scheduler that Qorvix found on their own reference boards. Some of them were good, but none were upstream.

52 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

So we end up carrying their debt as well as ours. I'd like to add a line to the Atlas BSP agreement with Arvenna: generic core-kernel changes must come with an upstream link, or a written reason why they can't go upstream.

53 · Elena ParkFramework

Is that realistic this late in the Atlas contract? The BSP 1.0 date is next week.

54 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Not for 1.0, no. But the contract has quarterly updates for three years. If we ask now, it can apply from the first quarterly update. Arvenna's own engineers have told me privately that they'd prefer to upstream more, but they need a customer to ask for it.

55 · Grace JungRelease Management

Then let's be that customer. I'll raise it with the partner management team so it goes through the right channel.

Proposal and Next Steps

56 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Let me try to pull this together. I'm hearing agreement on a few things, and real doubts on others, so let me propose a pilot rather than a policy.

57 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

For the next three months, on the Atlas kernel only, generic fixes must be posted upstream before or on the same day they land, with the link in the commit message. Security fixes and new hardware features are excluded. Every commit message must explain the problem and the fix, not just a ticket number. And changes in sensitive paths automatically get a security reviewer from another team.

58 · Jiho NamProduct Security

I'm happy with that. I'll send the list of sensitive paths to the tools team this week.

59 · Elena ParkFramework

Atlas is a good place to try it, because it's still early and nothing there is launch-critical yet. I'd like to see the numbers at the end before we talk about Orion.

60 · Brian ShinFramework

I'm still skeptical about speed, but a pilot is a fair way to find out. If it slows down a single urgent fix, I'll be the first to say so.

61 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

That's exactly the kind of feedback I want. I'll measure three things: how many patches we post, how many get merged, and how long it takes to land urgent fixes compared with today. Jiwoo, can you run an upstreaming office hour every Thursday for anyone who needs help with their first submission?

62 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

Happy to. I'll also write a one-page guide on how to format a patch for the mailing list, because that's where most people get stuck the first time.

63 · Grace JungRelease Management

And I'll add a field to the rebase tracker so we can see how many patches the pilot removes from our carry list. If the number goes down, that's the strongest argument for expanding it.

64 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

Great. Thank you all for a genuinely useful debate. We'll review the pilot results at the December session.

현재 상황

숫자부터 보겠습니다. Orion 커널은 안드로이드 공통 커널 위에 약 1,100개의 패치를 얹고 있습니다. 그중 약 200개는 업스트림에 절대 올라가지 않을 벤더 드라이버입니다. 나머지는 저희 것으로, 수정, 튜닝, 기능입니다. 그중 업스트림에 보낸 적이 있는 건 약 40개뿐입니다.

06 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

그리고 리베이스할 때마다 비용이 듭니다. 내부 테스트 브랜치를 6.1에서 6.6으로 옮길 때 엔지니어 세 명이 5주 동안 충돌을 해결했습니다. 그 충돌의 약 3분의 1은 업스트림이 이미 다른 방식으로 고친 버그를 고치는 패치에서 발생했습니다.

07 · Brian ShinFramework

그러니까 같은 버그를 두 번 고치고, 그다음 저희 버전을 버리느라 또 비용을 낸 거네요.

08 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

맞습니다. 그리고 저희 패치가 회사 밖으로 나간 적이 없어서 아무도 몰랐습니다.

리뷰 쪽을 보면, 커널 리뷰의 중간값은 업로드부터 승인까지 약 6시간입니다. 빨라 보이지만, 50개 리뷰를 샘플링해 보니 31개는 코멘트가 하나뿐이거나 없었습니다. 업스트림에서는 같은 종류의 패치가 보통 여러 차례 피드백을 받습니다.

10 · Elena ParkFramework

공정하게 말하면, 코멘트 없는 리뷰가 자동으로 나쁜 리뷰는 아닙니다. 가끔은 그냥 패치가 좋은 거죠.

11 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

맞는 말입니다. 하지만 지난 분기 회귀 8건을 커밋까지 추적해 보니 그중 6건이 코멘트 없이 승인됐습니다.

업스트림 우선을 지지하는 논거

12 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

제가 논거를 말씀드리겠습니다. 업스트림 우선이란, 일반적인 커널 수정이 저희 트리에 들어가기 전에 업스트림 메일링 리스트에 게시된다는 뜻입니다. 병합될 때까지 기다릴 필요는 없지만 게시는 돼야 하고, 그 링크가 커밋 메시지에 들어갑니다.

13 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

그렇게 하면 세 가지 일이 일어납니다. 첫째, 여러분이 바꾸는 코드를 작성한 사람들에게 리뷰를 받습니다. 둘째, 업스트림에 이미 더 나은 수정이 있으면 2년 뒤 리베이스 중에 저희가 발견하는 대신 며칠 안에 누군가 알려 줍니다. 셋째, 병합되면 그 패치는 저희 더미에서 영원히 사라집니다.

14 · Grace JungRelease Management

저는 세 번째가 중요합니다. 저희가 들고 있는 모든 패치는 다음 커널에서 누군가 또 들고 가야 하는 패치입니다.

15 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

구글도 이 방향으로 가고 있습니다. GKI에서는 명확한 업스트림 계획이 없는 벤더 훅을 이미 거부하고 있습니다. 그러니 어떤 의미에서는 어차피 결정이 내려지고 있는 셈입니다.

반대 논거

16 · Brian ShinFramework

누군가는 해야 하니 제가 반대편을 맡겠습니다. 제 걱정은 속도입니다. 출시를 막는 버그가 있으면 수정이 몇 주 걸릴 수 있는 공개 논의 이후가 아니라 오늘 밤 빌드에 들어가야 합니다. 업스트림 리뷰어들은 저희 출시일에 관심이 없습니다.

17 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

하지만 정책 내용은 그게 아닙니다. 게시하고 같은 날 반영하는 겁니다. 업스트림 리뷰는 병행해서 진행됩니다. 변경을 요청하면 나중에 개선된 버전을 가져오면 됩니다.

18 · Brian ShinFramework

그럼 어차피 반영할 거라면 얻는 게 뭔가요? 업스트림이 받아줄 때까지 여전히 그 패치를 들고 있잖아요.

19 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

피드백을 얻고, 그 패치가 저희 것이 아니게 되는 시한을 얻습니다. 지금은 그런 시한이 없어서 패치가 영원히 남습니다.

20 · Elena ParkFramework

제 걱정은 다릅니다. 저희 변경 중 일부는 제품 계획을 드러냅니다. 출시 3개월 전에 새로운 디스플레이 모드를 지원하는 패치를 게시하면 사람들이 그걸 읽고 저희가 뭘 만드는지 추측할 수 있습니다.

21 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP

정당한 우려이고, 그래서 저는 정책 범위를 새 하드웨어 기능이 아닌 일반적인 수정으로 한정하려 합니다. 스케줄러의 락 버그는 저희 로드맵에 대해 아무것도 드러내지 않습니다.

보안 관점

22 · Jiho NamProduct Security

여기서 모두에게 반론을 제기해야겠습니다. 보안 수정은 특별한 경우입니다. 커널에서 취약점을 찾았는데 단말이 패치되기 전에 수정을 공개 게시하면 공격자에게 어디를 봐야 할지 정확히 알려 주는 셈입니다. 그건 공개 리스트가 아니라 비공개 보안 절차를 거쳐야 합니다.

23 · Jiwoo LeeKernel & BSP

전적으로 동의합니다. 정책에서 보안팀을 거치는 건 명시적으로 제외하겠습니다. 그런 건 조율된 공개 일정을 따릅니다.

24 · Jiho NamProduct Security

좋습니다. 두 번째는 내부 리뷰에 관한 겁니다. 신뢰할 수 없는 입력의 파싱, 메모리 권한, 사용자 공간과 커널의 경계를 건드리는 변경은 다른 팀의 두 번째 리뷰어를 필수로 했으면 합니다. 그런 영역에서는 코멘트 없는 승인 하나로는 부족합니다.

25 · Brian ShinFramework

변경이 그 범주에 해당하는지 사람들이 어떻게 알죠? 엔지니어들이 자기 패치가 보안 경계를 건드린다는 걸 항상 아는 건 아니잖아요.

26 · Jiho NamProduct Security

경로로 할 수 있습니다. 민감한 디렉터리와 파일 목록이 이미 있습니다. 패치가 그중 하나를 건드리면 리뷰 도구가 자동으로 보안 리뷰어를 추가하게 할 수 있습니다.

좋은 리뷰란 무엇인가

27 · Daniel ChoKernel & BSP
